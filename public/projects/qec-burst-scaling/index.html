Scaling Laws of Transient Spatiotemporal Error Bursts in Topological Quantum Error Correction: An Exploratory Study

Yao-Ming Pai
Department of Computer Science, National Chengchi University
Report Code

Abstract

Quantum computers built from many unreliable physical qubits can, in principle, be turned into a reliable machine by combining thousands of them into error-corrected "logical" qubits, but this guarantee assumes errors occur uniformly across the chip. Real hardware does not always behave this way: occasionally a cluster of qubits becomes briefly and sharply noisier than the rest, for reasons that are not always known. This paper asks a simple question about such events: can we predict how dangerous one is just by comparing its size, duration, and strength to the size of the code protecting against it, without first knowing what caused it?

More precisely, quantum error correction (QEC) guarantees that, below a critical physical error rate, increasing code size suppresses logical error arbitrarily. Real superconducting hardware violates the uniform-noise assumption behind this guarantee in a specific way: rare, localized, transient events, here termed "bursts," temporarily elevate the error rate of a region of qubits. Google's most recent below-threshold demonstration (Google Quantum AI, 2025) reports one such mechanism, affecting roughly 30 qubits for about 400 microseconds, that the authors themselves cannot explain. We do not attempt to explain its physical cause. Instead, we ask whether a burst's danger can be predicted from a small number of dimensionless ratios: its spatial extent, duration, and strength, each measured relative to the code's own scale, regardless of cause. We build a phenomenological and circuit-level simulation pipeline on top of Stim (Gidney, 2021) and PyMatching (Higgott & Gidney, 2022, 2025), run thirteen controlled experiments on a rotated surface code, and show that relative spatial scale is a strong, three-times-independently-validated predictor of single-burst damage; discover, and validate on out-of-family random shapes, that burst orientation relative to the code's logical operator is an independent risk factor of comparable or greater magnitude that no prior model captures; and show, by testing against two independently-reported real hardware burst characterizations, that at real timescales a single burst event is essentially always catastrophic, reframing the open question from single-event severity to event frequency. We close by specifying the substantially different experimental design this reframing requires. All simulation code, experiment scripts, and raw results are available at https://github.com/AlanPai777/qec-burst-scaling.
