Abstract

Administrative regulations and forms at a university are typically scattered across dozens of office-specific web pages and PDF documents; answering a question such as "how do I apply for a leave of absence" requires assembling the complete procedure, the relevant form, and the office location and contact information for every signature field that form requires, information that, in our setting, spans seven offices across a nine-stop signature chain for a single form. Because the underlying documents in this class of application are often institutionally sensitive and cannot be sent to a closed-source API, we target a system that runs on a model deployable locally; we study it here on a corpus of public university regulations, as a prerequisite for applying the same architecture where documents cannot be shared. Conventional retrieval-augmented generation, built on dense embeddings and a reranker, struggles with this kind of query even under this constraint: a single response routinely took over ten minutes, and tended to reproduce regulatory text rather than the cross-office information the form actually required, while rebuilding the embedding index remained costly as the corpus grew. We present a question-answering system that instead relies on keyword search over the raw corpus and whole-page reading, rather than vector-based retrieval, with the language model itself deciding when to search and which pages to read, replacing the fixed retrieve-then-generate pipeline. In building this system, we found that the model's tool-calling behavior was itself unstable, frequently failing to invoke the tools a query required, a difficulty compounded by using a model small enough to deploy locally rather than a frontier system; we address this by drawing on and integrating several lines of agent-design research, namely chain-of-thought reasoning, ReAct-style action-observation loops, plan-and-solve decomposition, query routing, and self-evaluation, into a single multi-turn architecture. Along the way, we also observed that a sub-decision's reliability under model judgment tends to depend on whether an objectively checkable signal is available for it, rather than on how much prompt engineering is invested in it; resolving such decisions deterministically consistently improved reliability wherever we tested it. We evaluate the resulting system, without fine-tuning the underlying model, across several representative query types (complete procedures, single facts, form downloads, contact lookups, and cross-office compound queries), checking each response against manually-verified expected content such as named contacts, extension numbers, and form IDs, using a closed-source model as an upper-bound reference. Results show that the system reliably covers the required cross-office information, with traceable sources, on Gemma4:31b, a mid-sized, locally-deployed open-weight language model.
